# Training rules: one-page summary (for review)

These are the limits the app works inside when it builds a strength programme. The app takes each person's own days, time, kit, experience and sore spots. The coach can only choose options inside these limits and must explain why. Every rule is backed by research reviews or official guidelines (American College of Sports Medicine 2026 and 2009, NSCA, WHO), stored as thirteen evidence cards.

**1. How often.** Each main muscle area is trained at least twice a week. You can pick 1 to 6 days. One day still helps, and the app will say that two or more work better. Beginners do at most 4 strength days, and any extra days become optional easy days. People with 6–24 months of training get up to 5, and people with more than 2 years get up to 6. Leave about two days before working the same muscles hard again.
*Why:* twice a week is the guideline. For muscle, the total work in the week matters more than how it is split.

**2. The shape of the week.** 1–3 days: full-body sessions (people with 6+ months of training may also do upper / lower / full body on 3 days); with 2 or 3 days, the days are never back to back. 4 days: upper body, lower body, twice. 5–6 days (experienced people only): upper/lower plus full-body, or push/pull/legs. Every week covers all eight movement types: squat, hip hinge, lunge, push forward, push overhead, pull toward you, pull down, and core. Arm exercises are optional extras.

**3. How long.** Sessions last 15 to 90 minutes, whatever time the person has, and include a 3–5 minute warm-up. More time means more sets: about 6–9 hard sets in a short session, 9–14 in 30–44 minutes, and up to 24 in a long one. To fit a short session, two exercises that don't share a main muscle can be paired: you alternate their sets, and the other exercise's time counts as rest. Big exercises come first, then core and arms. The weekly amount (point 6) always wins: if the session size would go over it, the session gets fewer sets.

**4. How hard.** 2–3 sets per exercise for beginners, up to 4–5 for experienced people. Usually 8–15 reps (anything from 5 to 20 works). Pull-ups can go as low as 3. Each set stops 1–3 reps before you couldn't do another good one. Nothing is ever planned to total failure; experienced people may take the last set of a bodyweight or band exercise close to failure (0–1 reps left). While learning, stop 3–4 reps early.
*Why:* muscle grows with light or heavy work as long as sets are hard. Going all the way to failure adds little and costs recovery.

**5. Rest.** About 2 minutes after big exercises (1½–3 minutes) and about 1 minute after arms and core. You can change the timer.

**6. Weekly amount.** Hard sets per muscle area per week: beginners 6–12, then 8–16, then 10–20. An exercise that helps a muscle indirectly counts as half a set. The weekly amount goes up by at most 2 sets a week, and only after a good week.

**7. Getting harder: progress, hold or reduce.** When every set reaches the top of the rep range in two sessions in a row, the next step unlocks. That means a harder version, a slightly heavier weight or the next band. If reps are in range, stay and aim for one more. If reps drop below the range, or it feels "too hard", two sessions in a row, go one step back. A missed session is information, not failure: next week does not get harder, and nothing is doubled up. After two weeks or more off, restart a little lighter.

**8. Ladders.** Each move has a ladder of steps from easiest to hardest. The push-up goes wall → counter → knees → a low step (about 30 cm) → floor → feet raised → one-sided. The pull-up goes hang → shoulder-blade pulls → hold at the top → slow lowering → band-assisted → chin-up → pull-up → weighted. A short starting test puts each person on the right step, and they re-test every 4 weeks. You climb one step at a time.

**9. Easier weeks.** For people who have trained a while: an easier week every 4–6 weeks. Anyone gets one after two exhausting sessions in a row. An easier week keeps the same exercises at about half the sets, with no progression. Beginners don't get planned easier weeks in their first 8 weeks.

**10. Four versions of every session.** *Minimum*: main moves only, 1–2 sets each, and it still counts as done. *Normal*: the plan. *Full*: one extra set or exercise, within the weekly limit. *Travel*: bodyweight or a band only, same moves.

**11. Kit.** Kit options are none, bands, dumbbells, kettlebell, pull-up bar, bench and gym. The app only offers exercises you have the kit for. Every movement type has a no-kit option. Swaps are always the same movement type and never harder: never a higher level and never a higher step on the same ladder. The library holds 97 exercises. A second exercise for a movement is always a different ladder that counts as hard work: people with weights never get a chair sit-to-stand or wall sit as their second squat (a low-load second exercise only when sore spots leave nothing else and the weekly minimum needs it). Table rows, chair dips and doorway pull-up bars are not offered above 100 kg body weight (furniture and doorway bars are not made for it).

**12. Safety.** A known heart, metabolic or kidney condition, chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness, pregnancy or recent childbirth, or recent surgery or injury means: check with a doctor first, and start with minimum sessions. Once a doctor has cleared the person, the plan starts at the lowest level (two weeks of minimum sessions) and then progresses normally. Chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness, at any time, means no sessions until a doctor has cleared you. A sore knee, shoulder, wrist, lower back, elbow, hip, ankle or neck means exercises that load that area are swapped out; which exercises load which area follows fixed movement rules (every overhead press loads the shoulder and the neck, every lunge or step-up the knee, hip and ankle, and so on), checked automatically. Anyone who finds getting down to the floor and back up hard, or needs support for it, gets standing, seated or bench exercises only. Sharp or joint pain, on an exercise or at the check-in, means that exercise is swapped for one that loads none of the same areas, never back again in this programme, and nothing else changes that week. If the pain comes back, see a professional. When the check-in says recover or sessions are on hold, nothing progresses. This is wellness guidance, not medical advice.

**13. Your goal changes the plan.** Getting stronger (with 6+ months of training): the main lifts use 5–8 reps with 2–3 minutes' rest. Building muscle: weekly sets start near the middle of the range instead of the bottom. Losing fat: the strength work stays and aerobic time builds higher.

**14. Aerobic activity.** Everyone builds up towards 150–300 minutes a week of moderate activity (you can talk but not sing): walking, cycling, swimming or water walking, with low-impact options first for people 65+, with a high body weight or sore knees. It starts from the person's normal day (60–150 minutes a week) and rises by at most 15 minutes a week; fat-loss goals aim for 250. It is planned in minutes, never as a step count.

**15. People aged 65 and over, or with a very high body weight.** Exercises are standing, seated or on a bench, with no getting down to the floor (unless getting up from the floor is easy for them) and no head-down positions. People 65+ also do balance work on at least 3 days a week (a drill at the end of each session and short practice on other days), and lift their main squat quickly with a slow lowering (power).

**16. The plan remembers.** Each exercise slot keeps its step, its weight steps, set changes and recent results in the progress file. Two good sessions in a row can be in different weeks. Starting-test and re-test results are saved and set the step. An easier week is 40–60% of the week's sets. After two easier weeks in a row, if it still feels too hard, the plan steps down and suggests checking with a professional instead of giving easier weeks forever. Light practice moves (Y-T raises, hangs, shallow holds, balance drills) are never counted as hard sets. An own target of a first push-up or pull-up is trained twice a week, or the plan says plainly why it can't be.

**Gone for good:** the old rules from earlier plans. That means the short daily time limit, the daily push-up quota, the step target and the fixed calorie number. An automatic check makes sure they never come back.

**Honest limits:** the principles (twice a week, enough hard sets, small steps up) have solid evidence. The exact step sizes, the ladder order and the easier-week timing are careful judgements built on that evidence, not tested numbers. Each evidence card says which.
